Basic Hockey Terms (25)

Positions (Skater, Forward, Defenseman, Goalie)

A skater is any player who isn't the goalie. Skaters are forwards (a center and a left and right winger) and defensemen. A team typically dresses 12 forwards, 6 defensemen, and 2 goalies, and plays with five skaters and a goalie on the ice at full strength.

Forward Lines and Defense Pairs

Teams change players constantly. Forwards play in groups of three (a center and two wingers) called lines, and defensemen play in groups of two called pairs. A team usually dresses four forward lines and three defense pairs, with the top lines and pairs playing the most.

Zones (Offensive, Neutral, Defensive)

The two blue lines split the ice into three zones. A team's defensive zone is the end it's defending, its offensive zone is the end it's attacking, and the neutral zone is the middle.

Faceoff

How play starts or restarts. An official drops the puck between one player from each team, usually the centers, who battle for possession. Faceoffs happen at the start of each period and after stoppages.

Preseason

The exhibition games a team plays before the regular season starts, where coaches test lineups and look at prospects. They don't count in the standings.

Regular Season

The schedule of games that decides the standings. Each team plays 84 (used to be 82) games, and the standings decide which 16 teams (8 per conference) reach the playoffs.

Playoffs

The postseason tournament for the 16 teams that qualify. It is a series of best-of-seven rounds that ends with the Stanley Cup Final.

Home-Ice Advantage

The statistical edge a team gets from playing in its own arena.

Overtime/Overtime Loss

If a regular season game is tied after 60 minutes of play, it goes to a five-minute overtime with three skaters a side (four a side before 2015-2016), and then a shootout if it's still tied. A team that loses in overtime or a shootout still earns one standings point (an overtime loss, OTL), where a regulation loss earns none.

Penalty Shot/Shootout

A penalty shot is a free, uncontested attempt on the goalie, awarded when a player is fouled on a breakaway. A shootout is the series of one-on-one attempts used to settle a game that is still tied after overtime.

Shot on Goal/Missed Shot/Blocked Shot

The three things that can happen to a shot attempt. A shot on goal reaches the net and is either saved or scores. A missed shot goes wide of the net or hits the post or crossbar without going in. A blocked shot is stopped by a defending skater before it reaches the net.

Shot Types

How a shot was taken. A wrist shot is a quick sweeping release, a snap shot is a shorter, faster motion, and a slap shot uses a full wind-up for the hardest shot. A backhand uses the back of the blade, a tip-in redirects a shot or pass on purpose, and a deflection is a shot that changes direction off a player or stick on its way to the net. A wrap-around starts from behind the net. A poke is a short stab at the puck with the stick, a bat knocks the puck out of the air, a between-the-legs shot is taken through the shooter's own legs, and a cradle is a lacrosse-style shot where the puck is carried on the blade and flipped at the net.

Slot

The area in the middle of the ice in front of the net, between the faceoff circles. It is where the best scoring chances come from, so most high-danger shots are taken from the slot.

Point Shot

A shot from a player at the blue line, far from the net. A single point shot is usually a low-quality chance, but it can be effective through screens, tips, and rebounds.

Breakaway

A skater gets past every defender and has only the goalie to beat, with a clear path to the net. It is one of the highest-quality chances in the game.

Rebound

A rebound happens when the puck strikes the goaltender (or occasionally the goalpost or crossbar) and bounces back out into play, rather than being caught or frozen by the goalie.

Odd-Man Rush

A rush where the attackers outnumber the defenders, such as a 2-on-1 or 3-on-2. They create dangerous chances because the defense can't cover everyone.

Screen

A player standing in front of the goalie to block their view of the puck, making it harder to make the save.

Strength State

The number of skaters each team has on the ice, (5v5, 5v4, 4v5, etc.).

Even Strength (ES)

Play with both teams skating the same number of skaters, most commonly 5v5.

Power Play (PP)

When a team has more skaters on the ice than its opponent because of a penalty (5v4 is the most common). Together with the penalty kill, it makes up special teams.

Penalty Kill (PK)

When a team has fewer skaters on the ice than its opponent because of a penalty (4v5 is the most common), and is trying to keep the other team from scoring until the penalty ends. Together with the power play, it makes up special teams.

Empty Net/Pulled Goalie

A net with no goalie in it, usually because a trailing team takes its goalie off the ice and deploys an extra skater.

On-the-Fly Change

A line change made while play is happening instead of at a stoppage.

Back-to-Back

A team plays on two days in a row, so its second game comes on less rest.

Advanced Hockey Terms (12)

Shot Danger Zones (HDC/MDC/LDC)

Shots are sorted into three zones by where they were taken, measured from the center of the goal. A high-danger chance (HDC) is a shot attempt within 29 feet and between the lines from the faceoff dots to two feet outside the goalposts. A medium-danger chance (MDC) is a shot attempt from 29 to 43 feet, between the same lines. A low-danger chance (LDC) is a shot attempt from anywhere else: beyond 43 feet, from a wide angle outside the lines, or from behind the goal line.

Royal Road

An imaginary line running the length of the ice through the center of both nets. A shot or pass that crosses it tends to be more dangerous, since it forces the goalie to move laterally.

Off Wing

A shot taken from the side of the ice opposite a player's shooting hand (like a left shot playing the right wing), which tends to open up a better shooting angle toward the net.

Rush Shot

Any shot attempt within four seconds of an event in the neutral or defensive zone, with no stoppage in play in between.

Shift

The time from when a player gets on the ice to when they get off the ice.

Stint

A period of time when all the same players stay on the ice, ending as soon as any one of them changes. A shift can span several stints.

Zone Entry/Zone Exit

A player carrying, passing, or dumping the puck into the offensive zone (entry) or out of the defensive zone (exit). A "controlled" entry/exit keeps possession of the puck, while a dump-in/dump-out gives it up in exchange for territory.

Score State

The goal differential at a given point in a game (tied, up one, down two, etc.), which strongly influences how teams play.

Score Effects

The tendency for teams to change their style of play based on score state (trailing teams tend to attack more, leading teams tend to play more defensively).

Deployment Context

How a player is used, as opposed to how well they play: the quality of their teammates and opponents (QoT and QoC) and how often their shifts start in the offensive zone.

Quality of Competition (QoC)

A measure of how strong the opposing players a skater faces are on average, relative to the rest of the league.

Quality of Teammates (QoT)

A measure of how strong a player's own teammates are on average, relative to the rest of the league.

Hockey Rules (15)

Offside

A stoppage called when an attacking player crosses the offensive blue line ahead of the puck. Play restarts with a faceoff in the neutral zone.

Icing

A stoppage called when a team shoots the puck from behind the center red line all the way past the opponent's goal line and no one touches it. Play restarts with a faceoff in the zone of the team that iced it, and that team can't change players. A team that is shorthanded is allowed to ice the puck.

Goaltender Interference

Contact with the goalie in or around their crease that stops them from making a save. If an attacking player interferes, the goal doesn't count and a minor penalty can be called. If a defender pushes an attacker into the goalie, it isn't interference.

Penalty

A rule violation that sends a player to the penalty box, so their team plays a skater short (a power play for the other team). How long they sit depends on the type of penalty: minor, double minor, major, misconduct, or match.

Minor Penalty

A 2-minute penalty for common infractions like tripping or hooking. The penalized team plays a skater short, and the penalty ends early if the other team scores on the power play.

Common Minor Penalties

The most frequently called minors are tripping, hooking, slashing, holding, interference, high-sticking, roughing, cross-checking, delay of game (like shooting the puck over the glass from your own zone), and too many men on the ice.

Double Minor

A 4-minute penalty served as two back-to-back minors, usually for high-sticking that draws blood. A power play goal ends the first minor, and the second minor starts right after.

Major Penalty

A 5-minute penalty for more serious infractions, such as fighting. It stays in effect for the full five minutes even if the other team scores, so the penalized team can give up more than one power play goal.

Fighting

Two players dropping their gloves and throwing punches. Each fighter gets a 5-minute major, which cancels out, so teams usually stay at even strength. The player who starts the fight (the instigator) also gets a minor and a 10-minute misconduct.

Misconduct/Game Misconduct

A misconduct is a 10-minute penalty for unsportsmanlike behavior that sends the player off but lets their team stay at full strength with a replacement. A game misconduct ejects the player for the rest of the game, and their team stays at full strength unless another penalty came with it.

Match Penalty

The most serious penalty, called when a player tries to injure an opponent. The player is ejected, their team plays shorthanded for five minutes (another player serves the time), and the league reviews the play for a suspension.

Delayed Penalty

When a penalty is called but the team that was fouled still has the puck, play continues until the penalized team touches it. The fouled team usually pulls its goalie for an extra attacker during that time, and if it scores before the whistle, the penalized player may not have to serve their minor.

Coincidental Penalties

Equal penalties called on both teams at the same time, such as two minors or two majors. Both players sit but neither team gets a power play, so teams play 4v4 for coincidental minors.

Coach's Challenge

A coach can ask for a video review of a goal for offside or goaltender interference. If the call is overturned, the goal is wiped out, and if it stands, the challenging team is given a minor penalty for delay of game.

Last Change

The home team gets the final line change after every stoppage, so it can choose its matchups after seeing who the visiting team sends out. It is one reason home teams do slightly better.

Basic Hockey Stats (28)

Games Played (GP)

The number of games a player appeared in or a team has played.

Goal

The puck completely crossing the goal line between the posts and under the crossbar. Each goal adds one to the team's score, and the team with more goals at the end of the game wins.

Assist

Credit given to up to two players who touched the puck just before a teammate scored. Their touches have to be consecutive, so no opposing player can touch the puck in between. The last passer gets the primary assist and the one before that gets the secondary assist. Not every goal has assists, and some have only one.

Point

A goal or an assist: a player's points are their goals plus their assists. Not to be confused with the standings points a team earns for winning.

Game-Winning Goal (GWG)

The goal that gives the winning team the lead it never gives up. It is the goal that puts the winner one ahead of the loser's final total, so in a 4-2 win it is the team's third goal. A shootout winner counts as the game-winning goal.

Power Play Goal (PPG)

A goal scored by the team with the extra skater while it is on the power play.

Shorthanded Goal (SHG)

A goal scored by the team that is down a skater, which is rare and usually comes off a turnover.

Shots (SOG)

The number of shots on goal a player or team takes, usually shown as a total or per game. It leaves out missed and blocked shots, so it is smaller than the total number of shot attempts.

Shooting Percentage (SH%)

The percentage of shots on goal that result in a goal. Calculated as goals divided by total shots on goal.

Goals For/Goals Against (GF/GA)

The goals a team scores (GF) and the goals it allows (GA), either over a season or while a player is on the ice.

Goal Differential

A team's goals scored minus its goals allowed, including goals awarded for shootout wins.

Plus-Minus

A raw count of the goals scored for minus the goals scored against while a player is on the ice, not counting goals scored while their team has the power play. Simple, but credits and blames a player for every goal regardless of who else was on the ice.

Time on Ice (TOI)

The amount of game time a player spends on the ice, reported in either total minutes or as a per-game average.

Hits

A body check that separates an opponent from the puck. Hits are counted by the arena's scorekeeper, so the totals vary a little from arena to arena.

Blocks

A shot attempt stopped by a defending skater before it reaches the net.

Giveaways/Takeaways

A giveaway is a turnover where a player loses the puck to the other team, and a takeaway is when a player steals it. Both are judged by the scorekeeper, so they are somewhat subjective.

Penalty Minutes (PIM)

The total minutes a player accumulates from penalties they take. A minor adds 2, a major adds 5, and a misconduct adds 10.

Penalties Drawn/Taken

Penalties taken are the ones a player is called for, and penalties drawn are the ones called against the other team because of them. Drawn minus taken is a player's net penalties.

Faceoff Win Percentage (FO%)

The percentage of faceoffs a player or team wins.

Power Play Percentage (PP%)

The share of a team's power plays that end in a goal.

Penalty Kill Percentage (PK%)

The share of the penalties a team kills off without allowing a power play goal.

Saves/Shots Against (SA)

Shots against are the shots on goal a goalie faces, and saves are the ones they stop, so saves equal shots against minus goals allowed.

Save Percentage (SV%)

The percentage of shots on goal a goalie stops. Calculated as saves divided by shots on goal.

Goals Against Average (GAA)

The goals a goalie allows per 60 minutes played.

Shutout (SO)

A game in which a goalie allows no goals for the entire game. If two goalies split a game, neither gets the shutout.

Record (W-L-OTL)

A team's results as wins, regulation losses, and overtime losses. Wins are worth two standings points, overtime losses one, and regulation losses none.

Points Percentage (P%)

The percentage of available standings points a team has earned (2 for a win, 1 for an overtime or shootout loss, 0 for a regulation loss). Calculated as points divided by two times games played.

Counting Stat

A stat that adds up over a season, like goals or WAR, so a player who plays more games has more chances to add to it. A rate stat, such as goals per game or per 60, is scaled by playing time instead.

Advanced Hockey Stats (26)

Per 60 (/60)

A stat scaled to what it would be over 60 minutes of play, which is one full game of ice time: the count times 60 divided by the minutes played. It compares players with different ice time, which per-game numbers don't.

Relative (Rel)

A stat compared with the team's own result when the player is off the ice (the team's rate with them on the ice minus its rate with them off).

Corsi (CF%)

The share of all shot attempts (shots on goal, missed shots, and blocked shots) a team or player accounts for while on the ice. A common proxy for puck possession.

Fenwick (FF%)

The share of unblocked shot attempts (shots on goal and missed shots) a team or player accounts for while on the ice. (Corsi, but without blocked shots).

Individual Shot Attempts (iCF/iFF)

A player's own shot attempts. iCF counts every attempt (goals, shots on goal, misses, and blocks) and iFF leaves out blocks.

Scoring Chances (SCF/SCA)

Shot attempts taken from areas likely to produce a goal, counted for (SCF) and against (SCA) while a player is on the ice.

On-Ice Shooting %/Save % (oiSH%/oiSV%)

A team or player's shooting and save percentages while they're on the ice, as opposed to just their own individual shots or saves. The two components that make up PDO.

PDO

The sum of a team or player's on-ice shooting percentage and save percentage. A PDO far from 100 often signals unsustainable puck luck rather than true talent.

Zone Start Percentage (OZS%, NZS% or DZS%)

The percentages of a player's (or line's) zone starts, from faceoffs (shifts that begin on the fly are not counted), that were in the offensive zone (OZS%), the neutral zone (NZS%) and the defensive zone (DZS%).

Primary Points

A player's goals plus primary (first) assists, excluding secondary assists.

Individual Point Percentage (IPP)

The share of a team's goals while a player is on the ice that the player earned a point on, either scoring or assisting.

Expected Goals (xG)

The probability that a given shot results in a goal, based on shot location, type, and situational context. See the xG model above.

Individual Expected Goals (ixG)

The sum of the xG of the shots one player took themselves. Comparing it to their actual goals gives their goals above expected.

Expected Goals Against (xGA)

The sum of expected goals value from shots taken against a team or while a player is on the ice.

Expected Goals For % (xGF%)

The share of the expected goals in a player's or team's ice time that go to their own side. Calculated as xGF divided by the sum of xGF and xGA. Above 50% means they create better chances than they allow. Similar to Corsi, but each shot is weighted by its quality.

Goals Above Expected (GAx)

A shooter's actual goals minus the xG of their own shots. A positive number means they scored more than an average shooter would have on the same shots.

Finishing

How a player's goals compare with the xG of their own shots (goals above expected). A positive number means they scored more than an average shooter would have on the same shots. In WAR it is taken above replacement, and it can't separate real shooting talent from shot quality the xG model can't see.

Goals Saved Above Expected (GSAx)

A goalie's actual goals allowed subtracted from the number of goals an average goalie would be expected to allow on the same shots, based on xG.

Goals Saved Above Average (GSAA)

How many goals a goalie saved compared with an average goalie who faced the same number of shots: the league-average save percentage times their shots against, minus their goals against. It is like GSAx but doesn't account for how dangerous the shots were.

Regularized Adjusted Plus-Minus (RAPM)

A ridge-regression-based method for isolating a player's on-ice impact from the effects of their teammates and competition. See the RAPM model above.

Replacement Level

The performance baseline WAR is measured against. The production expected from a readily available fringe player.

Goals Above Replacement (GAR)

A player's goals above what a replacement-level player would have produced in the same role, before converting to wins. WAR is GAR converted to wins.

Wins Above Replacement (WAR)

A single number expressing how many wins a player added over a season compared to a replacement-level player. See the WAR model above.

Elo Rating

A sequentially updating team-strength rating that shifts after every game based on the result and how expected it was. See Elo ratings above.

Simple Rating System (SRS)

A team-strength rating fit across an entire season at once by minimizing error between predicted and actual goal margins. See SRS above.

Pythagorean Exponent

The exponent k in the Pythagorean formula, which estimates a team's win percentage from goals scored and allowed (goals for to the power k, divided by goals for to the power k plus goals against to the power k). WAR uses it to convert goals into wins. See the WAR model above.

Data Science Terms (17)

Sample Size

The number of observations behind a number. Small samples are noisy: a player's shooting percentage over 20 shots says little.

Percentile/Percentile Rank

The share of the qualifying pool that a player ranks above. A player in the 90th percentile ranks above 90% of the pool.

Standard Deviation

A measure of how spread out values are around their average. In a bell-shaped distribution, about two-thirds of values fall within one standard deviation of the average.

Regression to the Mean

The tendency for extreme results, good or bad, to move back toward average the next time, because part of them was luck. A player who shoots far above their xG one season will usually shoot closer to normal the next.

Ridge Regression

A regression technique that shrinks coefficient estimates toward zero to reduce overfitting, especially useful when predictors (like individual players) are numerous and collinear.

Collinearity

When two inputs move together so closely that a model can't tell which one is responsible. Linemates who are almost always on the ice together are the classic hockey case.

Covariate

An extra input to a model that isn't the thing being measured but can affect the outcome, such as home ice or score state in RAPM. Including it adjusts for it, so players aren't credited or blamed for it.

Prior

The starting estimate that a new estimate is pulled toward before the data is considered. In RAPM, it is built from the player's rating last season.

Gradient Boosting

A machine learning technique that builds a strong model out of many small decision trees, each one trained to correct the errors of the trees before it. XGBoost is a popular implementation. A model that sorts each input into a category (such as goal or no goal) is called a classifier.

One-Hot Encoding

A way of turning a categorical value into numeric columns a model can use, with one column per possible category that's set to 1 for the matching category and 0 for every other.

Gain (Feature Importance)

How much a feature's splits improve a tree model, used to rank how important each feature is. It favors features with many distinct values, so it can overstate their effect.

In-Sample vs. Out-of-Sample

In-sample results are measured on data the model was trained on and flatter it; out-of-sample results are measured on data it never saw, and are the honest test.

Overfitting

When a model learns the quirks and noise of its training data instead of the real patterns, so it looks great on data it has seen and worse on new data.

ROC Curve (Receiver Operating Characteristic)

A plot of a classification model's true positive rate against its false positive rate across every possible decision threshold, used to visualize how well it separates two outcomes.

AUC (Area Under the Curve)

The area under the ROC curve, condensing it into a single number: a measure of how well a classification model separates positive and negative outcomes, where 0.5 is no better than random and 1.0 is perfect separation.

Log Loss

A score for how close a model's predicted probabilities are to what actually happened, punishing confident misses hard. Lower is better.

Calibration

Whether predicted probabilities mean what they say. A common check is total predicted divided by total actual, where 1.00 is perfect.
