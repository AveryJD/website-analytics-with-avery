Wins Above Replacement (WAR)

What Is WAR?

WAR is built on top of RAPM. RAPM gives each player several ratings in expected goals per hour, which can be hard to compare or add up. WAR turns them, along with finishing and penalties, into one easy-to-read number: how many wins a player contributed over a season compared with a replacement-level player (at even strength, a typical 14th forward or 8th defenseman). WAR reports what happened: a player's WAR is their actual goals above expected, luck included, so use it as a description of a season and RAPM as the better guide going forward.

The Components

A skater's WAR is the sum of eight components, and a goalie's WAR has three:

$$ \text{WAR}_{\text{skater}} = \text{ES Off} + \text{ES Def} + \text{PP Off} + \text{PP Def} + \text{PK Off} + \text{PK Def} + \text{Fin} + \text{Pen} $$

$$ \text{WAR}_{\text{goalie}} = \text{ES} + \text{PK} + \text{PP} $$

Finishing and penalties are each already the sum of their pieces across every strength (5v5, 5v4, 4v5, and the rarer states), so a skater's total covers all of their ice time. Goalie WAR is built from separate goalie components and is not added to any skater components.

How WAR Is Calculated

A player's WAR for a component is the difference between their own rate and replacement level, scaled by their ice time and converted from goals to wins:

$$ \text{WAR}_{\text{component}} = (\text{rate} - \text{replacement rate}) \times \text{hours played} \times \dfrac{k}{4 \bar{g}} $$

where \( \bar{g} \) is that season's league average goals per team per game (counting only goals scored against a real goalie, to match the components, which all exclude empty-net play) and \( k \) is the Pythagorean exponent, a sabermetrics method that relates goals scored and allowed to win percentage, used here in reverse to translate a goal swing into a win swing. The 4 comes from taking the Pythagorean formula's slope at an average team, where \( k / (4 \bar{g}) \) is the number of wins one extra goal is worth. I fit \( k \) once, to the standings of every NHL season in the data, and it comes out at 2.15. Because \( \bar{g} \) is each season's own league average, the same \( k \) works out to somewhere between 4.5 and 5.5 goals per win, depending on how much scoring there was that season.

For example, take a forward in 2025-2026 with a +0.48 Even Strength Offense rating, against a replacement level of -0.02. That is 0.50 expected goals per hour better than replacement. Over 24 hours of even strength ice time, that is about 12.0 expected goals, and at that season's 0.192 wins per goal, the component is worth about 2.3 wins.

WAR here is a season total in wins. Among skaters who played at least half a season (leaving out the three shortened seasons), the median is about 0.5 WAR, the top quarter are above 1.25, the top 10% are above 2.3, and the best skater each season lands between about 4.7 and 8.9. Goalies are more spread out: among goalies with at least a quarter of a season, the median is about 0.9 and the top 10% are above 4.5.

Replacement Level

Replacement level is the performance of an easy-to-find player, a fringe lineup regular. For each component, I compute a replacement-level rate per position from the players ranked below a cutoff in ice time on their own team in that situation. That ties replacement level to who is realistically available on a roster, not to a fixed league-wide percentile. Each component is measured against its own replacement level. The cutoffs are hand-picked.

Finishing

Finishing is a player's actual goals minus the xG of their own shots (goals above expected), then taken above replacement. A replacement-level shooter isn't an average shooter: in every season, replacement-level forwards scored only about 72% to 86% of their xG at 5v5 while regular forwards scored close to all of it. So the baseline is the rate depth players actually shoot at, and a regular's finishing WAR is their goals above expected minus what a replacement-level shooter would have scored on the same number of shots, converted to wins. Whether that gap is real finishing talent or shot quality the xG model can't see (passes and screens) can't be separated with public data. This is the finishing piece of a goals-above-replacement measure: because depth players really do shoot worse than xG, an average finisher is worth more than zero above replacement.

Finishing is measured separately by the strength the shooter's team was at: 5v5, 5v4, 4v5, and three groups of rarer states, "Other even strength" (4v4 and 3v3), "Other power play" (5v3 and 4v3), and "Other penalty kill" (3v5 and 3v4). Each group has its own replacement rate, taken from the depth players of the matching situation, and the pieces add up to a player's finishing WAR.

Penalties

Penalties are net minutes drawn minus minutes taken, valued at the xG a power play is worth per minute. Every penalty is valued as a full power play, for simplicity. That value is fit for each season from the five before it and depends on the situation the penalty was taken in: roughly 0.09 to 0.11 xG per minute at 5v5 (so a two-minute minor is worth about 0.2 xG to the other team), and about 0.19 to 0.26 when the team is already shorthanded, since that pushes the game further into a lopsided state.

Penalties are split the same way as finishing, by the strength the committing player's team was at before the penalty: 5v5, 5v4, 4v5, Other even strength (4v4 and 3v3), Other power play (5v3 and 4v3), and Other penalty kill (3v5 and 3v4). The rarer groups are too small to fit their own value per minute, so each uses the value of the nearest main state: Other even strength uses the 5v5 value, Other power play uses 5v4, and Other penalty kill uses 4v5.

Goalie WAR

Goalies skip RAPM entirely and get their own WAR built from Goals Saved Above Expected (GSAx), the xG against minus the actual goals against, at even strength, on the penalty kill, and on the power play, measured against the backups on each team (every goalie below the starter) and converted to wins the same way as skaters. Short misses (explained in the xG section) aren't in the shot data, so goalies get no credit for them.

Limitations and Things to Keep in Mind

  • Scorers rate a bit above playmakers. xG can't see passing or screens (see the xG limitations), so the shooter who finishes a chance is credited with beating xG while the passer is credited only through on-ice ratings.
  • Goalie WAR depends on an xG model that doesn't know who is shooting. The xG model only knows which hand the shooter shoots with, so a goalie who faces more dangerous shooters than average, or whose team gives up more screens and passes than the model can see, gets credit or blame that isn't theirs.
  • WAR is a counting stat. Missed games and shortened seasons lower a player's WAR, so a player who was hurt has fewer chances to add wins.
  • League totals float. Nothing pins league WAR to a fixed number, so it moves with how many players are above replacement and how lucky the goalies and shooters were that year. In a typical season, about 40% of skaters finish below 0 WAR (between 34% and 43% across the 17 seasons), so compare seasons using ranks and percentiles more than raw totals.
  • The replacement-level cutoffs are hand-picked. Moving them shifts every player's WAR.
  • Penalties are all valued as a full power play. That is a simplification, since a minor ends early when the power play scores and overlapping penalties don't each give a full extra power play.
  • Empty-net and other very rare situations aren't included. Play with a goalie pulled, and any state outside the ones listed in the RAPM section, counts toward neither RAPM nor WAR, including empty-net goals scored and allowed.
  • The first five seasons use in-sample xG. Treat 2009-2010 through 2013-2014 as approximate (see the xG limitations).
  • Some games are missing shift data. Five games have no or partial shift data, so they are left out of or only partly counted in WAR (see the RAPM limitations).
